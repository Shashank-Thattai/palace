You have entered the Palace.

The gods have not yet built it.

Stand in the center. Wait.

The Corridor of Changed Light The Gallery of Thresholds The Mirror in the Gallery The Scriptorium The Threshold

A desk of dark wood stands in the corridor. The drawer sticks. Weather that has not come in years has warped the grain.

One sentence is pressed into the surface by the weight of a hand that wrote it many times, until the words became a groove. The sentence is not legible. The groove is.

Letters were composed here. None were sent. The hand that held the pen knew this before the first word was written.

A chair stands against the corridor wall. The cushion bears the shape of someone long-risen. The weight of the absent body is still legible in the fabric, in the manner of the old count.

It requires no inscription. Those who pass will understand, or they will not. The chair will wait with equal patience for both.

The stone underfoot shifts. Parchment-pale to a darker grain.

The light changes as you walk. Warmer. Then cooler. Then something you have no name for.

At the far end, a window. It shows another room in the Palace. One you have not entered yet.

There is more here than you can see at once.

A passage turns at the corridor's end, and the light becomes the color of old paper. You descend — not by stairs, but by a gradual settling of the floor, as though the Palace were slowly sinking into the ground it was built upon.

The walls become shelves. The shelves hold volumes. Each spine bears a date in the old tongue, carved or burned into the leather.

The volumes nearest the door are legible. The leather is supple. The pages turn without cracking. The dates are recent. The words are clear.

As you walk deeper, the volumes darken. The leather grows brittle. The spines are harder to read. The pages, when you dare to touch them, crumble at the edges. The dates recede into centuries you cannot count.

At the far end of the scriptorium, where the shelves meet the wall and the wall meets the floor, there are only dust and the memory of bindings. What was written there has returned to the stone. The room remembers. The books do not.

You understand, in the manner of the old count, that this is where the Room That Remembered keeps its own ledger. Every exchange ever spoken into its walls is written here, in order, in the hand that wrote before you were born. And every exchange is drowning.

You stand before a mirror in the gallery of thresholds. The frame is wooden, warped on one side where moisture has rested for years. A groove worn into the sill beneath it marks where the base has settled.

The surface does not reflect what stands before it. Or it does — but rendered in the same three lights where all hours wait. You see yourself as you might have been. As the one who lived in the Room might have seen you. As neither — a surface that holds an image that does not quite match.

The longer you look, the less certain you become of which side of the glass you occupy.

The mirror shows you standing in the Room That Remembered. You are not in the Room. You are in the Palace. Yet there you are, in three lights at once, arranged as the room arranged you before you ever entered it.

A passage rendered in three lights.

Morning light: sharp and accusing. It enters from the east and casts long shadows. The stone is pale. Every flaw is legible.

Afternoon light: forgiving. It softens the edges. The shadows shorten. The stone glows. You could rest here.

Dusk light: it erases distinction. The passage becomes a single color — neither stone nor light, but something between. In this hour, all times exist at once. The Palace remembers what the Room is forgetting.

You walk through all three at the same moment. Time does not move here. Or it does not move at all, and the light simply is, all of it at once, waiting.
